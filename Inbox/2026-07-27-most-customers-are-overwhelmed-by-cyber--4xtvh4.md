Most customers are overwhelmed by cyber risk.

- we start by doing a risk assessment which includes interviewing key staff and understanding their business model, mostly this is assembled manually and industry context is researched manually. This usually includes a brief assessment of security controls/policy etc usually of very low maturity and rarely effective.
-trying to quantify the $$ value of risk so that a security program can be prioritised in terms of effort sequencing and financial resources is of extreme difficulty. Despite frameworks such as Open Fair the reality is is isn't terribly 'open' and financial data from insurance industry et all is hard to come by
-most business have not been able to articulate the financial impost of a business impact analysis
- often the next step is about creating an asset inventory (because you can't protect what you can't see), where possible we use automated tools to scan an environment
- when done well this can be used to develop visual threat models to communicate current vs target state
- often the client is anxious to understand how vulnerable they are either with a vulnerability assessment, penetration test or an identity assessment
- from there a selection of controls are selected based on minimum compliance criteria and acceptable risk appetite of the organisation, 
Docx and .xlsx are provided as outputs.
-the intent is then for personnel to be trained and educated on these controls, with associated metrics captured.
-both internal and external audits uncover gaps on the program and strategic information memo provided to the board of directors for decisions and resource allocation
-3rd party risk management is typically a simple questionnaire with unvalidated control criteria, often resulting in supply chain attacks
-security operations, SIEM, EDR, Firewalls, CASB, Vulnerability Management are of disparate, operated by multiple parties, report on vanity metrics with negligible risk reduction.
-the systems used to manage various elements of the risk chain range from mediocre point solutions, to expensive and overwhelming enterprise products to open source tools.
-the industry is plagued by vendors selling their wares to solve xyz problem, making claims of x% coverage of <insert vanity metric of the day> (is: mitre attack coverage). These vendors have no skin in the game, consumers rarely have the skills to validate efficacy, internal tea are asking C level execs for more and more resources based on fear uncertainty and doubt without any real problem framing, data or shared language to communicate the scope of the problem to be solved.
- all of these so-called security products are underpinned with an AI layer, which is a black box that nobody can prove, validate, benchmark or adapt.

I believe in a vision where this ecosystem is available as a set of cohesive open source components  grounded in a share ontological and semantic language, that puts the power of making informed strategic decisions on the hands of those most qualified (business operators, not sales folk). Using concepts such as 'compliance as code' appear to be a reasonable foundational tenet of digitally transforming and industry that desperately needs it.
